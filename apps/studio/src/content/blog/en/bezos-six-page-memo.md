"Writing a good narrative is evidence of deep, structured thinking. Bullet points hide sloppy thinking." 📝

Jeff Bezos (1964–), founder of Amazon, the world's largest e-commerce and cloud platform, and pioneer of customer-obsessed management philosophy. Having reshaped global industries through the e-commerce revolution and AWS (Amazon Web Services), he introduced a radical management ritual to maximize organizational decision-making speed and quality: a total ban on PowerPoint presentations in executive meetings and the implementation of 6-page narrative memos.

In 2004, Bezos completely banned PowerPoint slides filled with bullet points in S-Team executive meetings. He believed that flashy, summarized slides allowed presenters to hide logical flaws behind rhetoric while inducing superficial consensus among attendees. Instead, he required teams to author structured, 6-page narrative memos containing rigorous cause-and-effect prose. Meetings began with a 30-minute 'study hall' where all attendees read the memo in total silence before discussing it. This 30 minutes of silence was not mere reading time; it served as a powerful cognitive synchronization platform, aligning every attendee's intellectual depth and context perfectly.

In today's post, we examine the brain science underlying Jeff Bezos's '6-page narrative memo reading' method and present a 3-step practical guide for modern individuals to analyze complex problems deeply and arrive at optimal decisions.

## How Reading Narrative Memos Maximizes Prefrontal Cortex Judgment

Fragmented bullet points offer an illusion of clarity, but in reality, they often conceal logical gaps and obscure complex cause-and-effect relationships. Conversely, deeply reading complete narrative prose strongly activates the brain's prefrontal cortex. The prefrontal cortex is the highest-level executive control center responsible for analyzing cause-and-effect, utilizing working memory, and weighing pros and cons logically. Silent reading increases the information processing density in the prefrontal cortex, preventing errors in judgment and enabling seamless cognitive synchronization across meeting participants.

---

## Caution & Tips: Avoiding Mere Bullet Lists & Establishing Clear Causality

When authoring a 6-page narrative memo, avoid simply stretching short bullet points into full sentences. The narrative must articulate clear cause-and-effect relationships (e.g., Problem A triggers Outcome B, which can be resolved via Solution C to generate Business Value D). Writing that merely lists facts without logical connections increases cognitive load and brain fatigue; therefore, maintain a rigorous logical structure supported by evidence.
