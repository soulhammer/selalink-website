"Man is condemned to be free." ☕

Jean-Paul Sartre (1905–1980) was a prominent French existentialist philosopher, playwright, and novelist who reshaped 20th-century Western thought. He authored monumental works such as 'Being and Nothingness' (L'Être et le Néant), 'Nausea' (La Nausée), and 'No Exit' (Huis Clos), dissecting human agency and existence, and was awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1964.

In 1940s occupied Paris, Sartre and his lifelong companion Simone de Beauvoir made famous Saint-Germain-des-Prés cafés—such as Café de Flore and Les Deux Magots—their personal office and study. Leaving unheated apartments behind, Sartre sat at his reserved café table daily from 9 AM to 12 PM and 3 PM to 8 PM, writing quietly amidst gentle ambient noise, the aroma of coffee, and human warmth.

For Sartre, the moderate café hum and solitude functioned as an environmental anchor that optimally aroused cortical attention and fueled executive function. Through this reserved seat routine, he maintained deep immersion despite external turmoil, producing the foundational texts of existentialism.

In this post, we explore the creative cognitive mechanisms behind Sartre's café writing routine and introduce practical environmental cognitive anchoring strategies for modern life.

## 1. Spatial Cognitive Anchoring and Conditioned Focus Reflex

Our brain possesses strong contextual plasticity, linking specific spaces with the actions performed within them. For instance, beds are associated with sleep, and dining tables with eating.

Sartre's insistence on reading and writing exclusively at a specific table in a specific café imprinted <strong>'Spatial Cognitive Anchoring'</strong> onto his frontal lobe. Simply sitting at that seat triggered a conditioned reflex, automatically activating 'focus mode' without warmup delay. Combined with time-blocking that strictly bounded morning and afternoon focus sessions, his peak concentration efficiency was consistently preserved.

## 3. Prohibit Non-Target Behaviors at Your Reserved Seat

For spatial cognitive anchoring to remain effective, you must maintain a strict rule: perform 'only target behaviors (studying, writing)' in that designated space. If you surf the web, answer calls, or play games at your reserved seat, the brain's space-behavior link weakens and breaks the anchoring effect. When taking a break, always step away and rest elsewhere.
