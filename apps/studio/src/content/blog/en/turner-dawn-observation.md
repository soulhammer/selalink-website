Joseph Mallord William Turner (1775–1851), a prominent 19th-century British Romantic painter known as the 'Painter of Light' for his ability to capture subtle changes in light and atmosphere, woke up earlier than others every dawn to observe the majestic sunlight and cloud scattering at sunrise. This dawn light observation reset his circadian rhythm and provided him with profound artistic inspiration.

In today's post, we introduce the cognitive scientific principles behind painter Turner's dawn sunrise observation routine.

This routine is not a mere one-time habit, but is based on a thorough scientific mechanism that reduces prefrontal cognitive overload and strongly engraves long-term memory synaptic circuits. Based on verified historical facts and cutting-edge neuroscience research, we analyze concrete practical methods.

## 1. Gradual Changes in Light Stimulating the Visual Cortex

The sky at sunrise undergoes a rapid change in color temperature within a short time, shifting from low blue light to bright natural daylight. This gradual visual stimulation highly trains the contrast sensitivity of the brain's visual cortex.

Training oneself to elaborate visual information in the mind and engrave it in the 'mind's eye'—rather than immediately painting the stimulus on canvas—connects the parietal lobe and frontal lobe of the brain, enhancing visuospatial working memory. This serves as the neurological foundation for capturing the essence of light and nature in three dimensions and unleashing creative brilliance.

## 3. Caution: Do Not Gaze Directly at Intense Sunlight

The core of Turner's routine is not to look directly at the strong light source of the sun. It is to observe the soft scattered light spreading through the faint sky, mist, or clouds just before sunrise. To prevent retinal damage, avoid looking directly at intense sunlight for extended periods.
