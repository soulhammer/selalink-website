"A character that cannot be comprehended in a single touch without moving the fingertip is not a true letter." ⠃

Louis Braille (1809–1852) was a legendary inventor who, despite losing his sight in early childhood, gifted the freedom of reading and writing to visually impaired people worldwide, sparking a monumental revolution in human knowledge accessibility. Having lost his sight in both eyes at age three due to an infection following an accident with an awl in his father's harness shop, he later entered the Royal Institute for Blind Youth in Paris. There, he encountered Captain Charles Barbier's 'Night Writing' system—a complex 12-dot military code designed for reading messages in the dark without lights.

However, young Braille quickly recognized a fatal flaw in the 12-dot system: its matrix was too large to be encompassed under a single fingertip, forcing the finger to move up and down, which drastically slowed reading speed and caused cognitive overload. Between the ages of 12 and 15, he systematically tested his own tactile spatial acuity daily, ruthlessly pruning redundant dots until he derived the optimal 6-dot matrix (2 columns by 3 rows) that could be instantly captured in a single tactile scan. This relentless simplification routine became the engine of an information revolution that allowed visually impaired individuals to effortlessly read text, mathematics, and musical scores.

In today's post, we analyze the brain science behind Louis Braille's '6-dot simplification and chunking' routine and present a 3-step practical action plan for modern individuals to streamline complex workflows and prevent cognitive overload.

## 1. Neuroscience of Working Memory Chunking and Tactile Spatial Acuity Limits

Human working memory and the somatosensory cortex face clear physical and cognitive limitations regarding high-resolution information processed in a single instance. The 12-dot system exceeded the spatial resolution of tactile receptors on fingertips, placing unnecessary spatiotemporal scanning overload on the brain. Conversely, Braille's 6-dot cell aligns perfectly with the brain's 'Chunking' mechanism—grouping raw inputs into single recognizable units. Chunking complex workflows into 3 to 5 core units drastically reduces prefrontal cognitive load and accelerates information processing speed.

## 3. Caution & Tips: Ruthless Elimination of Redundant Elements & Preserving the Core

The biggest hurdle in simplification is keeping unnecessary steps out of fear that 'they might be needed later'. Attempting to accommodate every edge case or secondary option makes systems complex again and degrades cognitive flexibility. Just like the 6-dot cell captured instantly under a single fingertip, aggressively prune away secondary elements, leaving only the vital essence that drives 80% of results.
