"We should look for someone to eat and drink with before we look for something to eat and drink." 🍇

Epicurus (341–270 BC) was the Hellenistic Greek philosopher who defined true happiness as 'Ataraxia' (a mind free from anxiety) and 'Aponia' (a body free from pain). Though modern culture often misinterprets his philosophy as indulgent hedonism, Epicurus actually lived an exceptionally frugal, self-disciplined life focused on tranquility.

Outside Athens, Epicurus established 'The Garden' (Kepos), an egalitarian community open to all regardless of social status or gender. Every afternoon, Epicurus and his close friends shared simple meals of water, barley bread, olives, and an occasional morsel of cheese, engaging in philosophical conversations anchored in deep friendship (Philia). He famously proclaimed, "Send me a little pot of cheese, that I may be able to indulge myself whenever I wish." In today's post, we dissect the cognitive neuroscience behind Epicurus's garden dining routine and how modest, secure social bonds foster enduring mental peace.

## 1. Psychological Safety and the Bonding Effects of Oxytocin and Serotonin

A small, trusted group where thoughts can be shared without judgment provides profound 'Psychological Safety' to the brain. Engaging in meaningful conversations within this secure circle triggers the hypothalamus to release oxytocin, the hormone responsible for emotional bonding and stress alleviation. Oxytocin dampens hyper-reactivity in the amygdala and reduces cortisol levels, mitigating cognitive fatigue and enhancing emotional stability.

## 3. Caution: Excluding Artificial Stimuli to Focus on Authentic Conversation

A common mistake when hosting social gatherings is over-indulging in lavish meals or alcohol, which creates artificial excitement. While alcohol and gluttony cause sudden dopamine spikes, they are rapidly followed by depletion, amplifying chronic anxiety. Just as Epicurus set his table with basic bread and water, keeping gatherings frugal clears dopamine receptor clutter, allowing the brain to perceive gentle, lasting serenity (Ataraxia).
