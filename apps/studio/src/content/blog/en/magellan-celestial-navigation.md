"The sea has no maps or roads, but the stars and logs never lie." 🧭

In 1519, Ferdinand Magellan embarked on humanity's first circumnavigation of the globe with five ships and over 270 crew members. Amid storms, starvation, scurvy, and the extreme uncertainty of the vast Pacific Ocean, what was his secret to steering the fleet safely through uncharted waters? 📜

No matter how exhausted he was, Magellan stepped onto the deck every night to measure celestial positions using a cross-staff and compass. He then sat in his cabin to cross-check daytime observations—wind direction, weather conditions, and distance traveled—painstakingly completing his navigation log. This nightly routine of rigorous data verification served as an intellectual compass that suppressed emotional panic and minimized decision-making errors.

In today's post, we examine the cognitive neuroscience behind Ferdinand Magellan's 'nightly celestial observation and cross-checking routine,' focusing on metacognition enhancement and error-monitoring networks.

## 1. Activation of the Prefrontal Error-Monitoring Network (ACC) and Metacognition

In environments governed by extreme uncertainty and peril, human error can be fatal. Nightly cross-checking of observation data against sailing records activates the brain's Anterior Cingulate Cortex (ACC) and prefrontal network, sharpening error-monitoring capabilities.

This rigorous process of objectification prevents cognitive biases and speculation, maximizing metacognition—the ability to assess one's own state and knowledge objectively—so that optimal decisions can be made under pressure.
