"If you cannot preserve the human spirit, no expedition can ever be accomplished." ❄️

Sir Ernest Shackleton, a legendary British Antarctic explorer and the embodiment of crisis management and leadership, set sail in 1914 aboard the 'Endurance' with the goal of completing the first land crossing of the Antarctic continent. However, before even reaching their landing site, the ship became trapped in dense polar pack ice and was ultimately crushed and sunk. Completely cut off from civilization, the crew was forced to drift on ice floes for two years (approx. 635 days) in sub-zero polar cold.

Yet, amid terrifying isolation and despair, Shackleton achieved a miracle: bringing all 28 crew members back alive without a single fatality. While numerous polar expeditions throughout history dissolved under plummeting morale, internal dissension, and isolation-induced depression, Shackleton's crew maintained perfect cohesion due to his 'Nightly Tent-Visiting & Morale Check' routine.

Every night on the ice, Shackleton personally visited each tent, sharing hot tea or playing cards with his men. He meticulously observed subtle facial expressions, vocal tones, and emotional cues. When he detected signs of anxiety or discontent, he immediately engaged in deep 1-on-1 dialogue to ease their worries and restore morale. In today's post, we introduce the cognitive principles of establishing Psychological Safety and crisis leadership embedded in Ernest Shackleton's nightly dialogue routine.

## 1. Formation of Psychological Safety and Suppression of Emotional Contagion

Under conditions of extreme stress and isolation, the amygdala—the brain region responsible for fear and anxiety—becomes hyperactive, leading to surges in depression, hostility, and declining morale. In this state, an individual's despair can rapidly spread throughout a group via 'Emotional Contagion', causing the organization to collapse from within.

Ernest Shackleton's nightly 1-on-1 routine consistently provided his crew with 'Psychological Safety', calming the prefrontal cortex. Daily evening interactions with their leader signaled to the crew's brains that they were protected, lowering cortisol (stress hormone) levels and stimulating oxytocin release to strengthen interpersonal bonds. This served as a scientific organizational care system that identified and defused friction before it could escalate.

## 3. Focus on Genuine Listening Rather Than Formal Performance Evaluations

Shackleton's routine was not a formal performance review or inspection. Rather than delivering one-sided orders or lectures, the core of his approach was empathetic listening to his men's small daily concerns or underlying fears. When applying this method in modern organizations or relationships, maintaining a sincere attitude that accepts emotions without judgment is essential.
