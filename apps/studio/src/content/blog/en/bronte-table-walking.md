Charlotte Brontë, the author of 'Jane Eyre,' the novel that presented the greatest shock in 19th-century English literary history. She lived a life of severe isolation and poverty with her sisters Emily and Anne Brontë in a remote vicarage in Haworth, Yorkshire. In an isolated environment without proper intellectual stimuli or external exchanges, how did these three sisters simultaneously complete masterpieces like 'Jane Eyre' and 'Wuthering Heights'?

As powerful as Branson's persistent note-taking was the Brontë sisters' 'Table Walking' habit. After writing separately in isolation during the day, the sisters gathered at 9 PM and began walking in a circle around the large dining table. While walking, they read aloud today's plots, characters, and dialogues, pouring sharp critique and ideas into each other's works. This unique night walking collaboration was a magical gateway to correct viewpoints and maintain creative energy.

Today, BuildSelf introduces the neuroscientific principles of Charlotte Brontë's 'table walking' routine and how modern people can adapt this to meetings or idea retrieval.

## 1. Creative Neurotransmitters and Mirror Neurons Enhanced by Walking

The act of physically moving and walking is a catalyst that maximizes the brain's cognitive performance. According to Stanford University clinical research, creative divergent thinking improves by over 60% while walking compared to sitting. Walking increases blood flow and oxygen supply to the brain, activating nerve growth factors to break static thought patterns.

Additionally, sharing stories while receiving sharp critiques and ideas from others engages the brain's <strong>'Mirror Neurons'</strong> system. By simulating another's perspective to look at one's own creation, it strongly suppresses the <strong>'Tunnel Vision'</strong> phenomenon where writers get trapped in their narrow thoughts, raising the objective persuasiveness and structural completeness of the work.

## 3. The Inevitable Necessity of Objectifying Feedback for Logical Check

    <p class="text-slate-700 dark:text-slate-300 text-sm m-0 leading-relaxed">

If the sisters had stopped at praising each other's works, dense masterpieces like 'Jane Eyre' would never have been born. A receptive mind that listens without getting defensive when others point out logical flaws or plot holes is necessary for the Brontë sisters' correction mechanism to work perfectly.
