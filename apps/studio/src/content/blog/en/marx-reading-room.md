Karl Marx (1818 - 1883) was one of humanity's greatest economic thinkers, breaking down the boundaries of philosophy, economics, and history to rigorously analyze the structural contradictions of capitalist society. How was his monumental masterpiece, 'Das Kapital', which sent intellectual shockwaves through human history, produced through his relentless mental discipline? 🏛️

The foundation of Marx's vast academic infrastructure was laid in the Reading Room of the British Museum in London. For nearly 30 years during his London exile, he entered the library at 9:00 AM sharp when the doors opened and stayed buried in books until closing time at 7:00 PM, spending over 10 hours every day. From his assigned seat beneath the massive circular dome, Marx meticulously combed through worldwide railroad statistics, agricultural ledgers, and parliamentary blue books. Going far beyond passive reading, he copied key excerpts into notebooks and appended critical commentaries, creating an immense series of excerpt notebooks throughout his life. This relentless, structured study routine provided the bedrock for his historic legacy.

In this post, we explore the neuroscientific principles behind Karl Marx's British Museum reading routine and introduce an analog deep reading habit to filter out digital noise and grasp core truths.

## Why Did 10 Hours of Deep Reading Under the Dome Enable Intellectual Reconstruction?

According to neuroscientific research, the ability to integrate fragmented knowledge from diverse fields at a high level originates from the activation of the brain's Association Cortex and frontal lobes. Marx's daily 10-hour immersion in analog texts at the British Museum—completely isolated from external distractions while compiling hand-written excerpt notebooks (Exzerpiorhefte)—was a neurological Deep Work process that multidimensionally reconfigured neural networks.

---

## Beware of Passive Reading and the Illusion of Digital Collection

Simply saving numerous digital articles or PDFs and skimming them visually leaves only superficial brain stimulation, failing to build long-term memory or complex conceptual connections. Without active annotation—copying key passages by hand and asking critical questions like Marx did—information never converts into true intellectual capital.
