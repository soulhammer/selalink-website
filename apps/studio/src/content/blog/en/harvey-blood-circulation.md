17th-century English physician and Royal Court doctor William Harvey (1578–1657) revolutionized medicine by discovering the systemic circulation of blood pumped through the body by the heart. Published in his 1628 landmark treatise 'De Motu Cordis', Harvey's discovery is regarded as the birth of modern physiology.

For nearly 2,000 years, Western medicine was dominated by Galen's dogma—the unproven belief that blood was continuously manufactured in the liver and consumed by tissues. Instead of accepting traditional textbooks on faith, Harvey adopted a rigorous habit: calculating exact quantitative volumes of heart stroke volume and blood flow.

Harvey mathematically demonstrated that in just one hour, the heart pumps more blood than the entire weight of a human body. Since the liver could not possibly manufacture such immense quantities of blood every hour, he deduced that blood must flow in a continuous closed circuit. This habit of calculating hard quantitative numbers dissolved centuries of dogma and sharpened left-brain analytic clarity.
