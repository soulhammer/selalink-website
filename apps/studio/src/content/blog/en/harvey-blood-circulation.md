"Truth lies not in the tradition of textbooks, but in the objectively measured quantities of nature." 🫀

William Harvey (1578–1657) was the royal physician to King Charles I and the father of modern physiology who introduced the revolutionary concept of the circulatory system. Published in his landmark 1628 treatise, *Exercitatio Anatomica de Motu Cordis et Sanguinis in Animalibus* (*De Motu Cordis*), Harvey demonstrated that blood pumps continuously through a closed circuit driven by the heart.

For nearly 2,000 years, Western medicine had been ruled by Galen's ancient dogma, which asserted that blood was continuously manufactured in the liver and consumed by body tissues as fuel. Refusing to succumb to blind authority, Harvey applied a groundbreaking mathematical methodology. By calculating the heart's stroke volume (approx. 2 ounces) and multiplying it by average heart rate, Harvey proved that the heart pumps over 500 pounds of blood in a single hour—more than triple the weight of an entire human body. Because the liver could physically never produce such a massive volume of blood in 60 minutes, Harvey proved that blood must circulate endlessly through veins and arteries.

In today's post, we dissect the cognitive neuroscience behind William Harvey's quantitative verification routine and present a 3-step action guide to eliminate cognitive illusions and master data-driven decision-making.

## 1. Mathematical Quantification and Left Parietal Lobe Logic Control Mechanisms

Engaging in precise numerical tracking rather than relying on vague intuition strongly stimulates causal computational networks across the <strong>Left Parietal Lobe</strong> and the <strong>Prefrontal Cortex</strong>. This process of <strong>Mathematical Quantification</strong> systematically strips away confirmation bias and ancient entrenched illusions. By downregulating hyper-arousal in the amygdala caused by subjective guessing, quantitative tracking engages top-down prefrontal executive function, establishing rigorous logical clarity.
