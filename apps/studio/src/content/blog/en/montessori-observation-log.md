"When we observe children's behavior as it truly is, only then does the great potential within them reveal itself." 📝

Maria Montessori (1870–1952) was Italy's first female physician and a revolutionary educator who transformed early childhood education worldwide. Graduating from the University of Rome Medical School in 1896, she established the first 'Children's House' (Casa dei Bambini) in Rome's San Lorenzo district in 1907, pioneering an educational philosophy that departed from rote learning to cultivate children's innate drive for self-directed discovery.

The key to Montessori's groundbreaking insights into child development was her rigorous 'objective observation log'. When observing children, she eliminated emotional biases and adult-centric subjective judgments entirely. Viewing interactions through the precise lens of a medical researcher, she documented pure facts—focusing strictly on nouns and action verbs—recording under what environmental conditions children concentrated and acted. Stripping away subjective speculation, this observation habit served as the ultimate cognitive tool to see the core of any situation.

In today's post, we explore the principles of cognitive objectification embedded in Maria Montessori's observation routine and present practical methods for modern individuals to eliminate confirmation bias and reframe their work and personal growth.

## 1. Cognitive Objectification Overcoming Confirmation Bias and Facing Reality

We often fall into 'confirmation bias', drawing hasty conclusions based on subjective speculation derived from emotions or past habits.

Dr. Montessori's observation routine strengthens cognitive control networks in the prefrontal cortex, providing a <strong>'cognitive bracketing'</strong> effect that temporarily suspends emotional judgment. Separating facts from subjective feelings when recording data brings the true root causes of misunderstandings and errors clearly into focus.

## 3. Record Only Pure Facts Without Evaluations or Emotional Adjectives

The core of Montessori observation is describing strictly sensed raw facts—such as 'closed eyes 30 times in 10 minutes' instead of 'looks tired'. Rather than relying blindly on willpower, designing physical environments and habit triggers that naturally evoke objective recording is key to long-term consistency.
