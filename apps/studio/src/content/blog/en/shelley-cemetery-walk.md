"Solitude is my most cherished companion, the secret womb where imagination is born."

Mary Shelley, author of the immortal masterpiece 'Frankenstein' that opened the doors to Gothic horror and modern science fiction, harbored a solemn walk routine from early childhood: visiting the graveyard at St Pancras Old Church daily for contemplation.

Mary Shelley spent hours sitting by the gravestone of her mother, the famed feminist philosopher Mary Wollstonecraft who died shortly after Mary's birth, reading books and practicing writing. To Mary, the cemetery was not a place of terror, but a peaceful open-air sanctuary where she could ponder life, death, identity, and human boundaries. The solemn quietude beside her mother's grave formed the creative crucible from which 'Frankenstein' emerged when she was just 18 years old.

Here we explore the scientific principles of cognitive awe and deep imagination inspired by Mary Shelley's 'Cemetery Walk' routine.

## 1. Cognitive Awe and Reflective Brain Network Activation in Solemn Spaces

Environmental psychology shows that entering quiet spaces filled with history and solemnity—such as graveyards, ancient cathedrals, or historic ruins—triggers a unique cognitive emotion: 'Awe'.

Awe quiets the brain's ego-centric default mode network responsible for daily worries and self-centered stress, activating the reflective networks dedicated to philosophical contemplation. Walking in solemn tranquility calms the sympathetic nervous system and frees the brain from visual noise, making it far easier to conjure original, deep imaginative ideas.

## 3. Seek Out Quiet, Non-Commercial Historical or Natural Spaces

For Mary Shelley, the cemetery was a quiet sanctuary to reflect on life and death. You don't necessarily need a graveyard—seek out old forest paths, quiet historic grounds, serene libraries, or secluded temples. The key is offering your brain a space completely separated from commercial noise and daily social friction.
