"Perfection is achieved, not when there is nothing more to add, but when there is nothing left to take away." 💻

Steve Wozniak (1950–), the legendary engineer who co-founded Apple with Steve Jobs and single-handedly designed the Apple I and Apple II—the first personal computers connecting a keyboard and monitor directly to a mainboard. While contemporary engineers relied on over 100 chips to build primitive machines, Wozniak accomplished the same feats using barely 40.

His secret lay in his daily 'Minimalist Refactoring Routine'. During quiet late-night sessions at home or his desk at HP, Wozniak reviewed completed schematics and systematically challenged himself to remove a chip, gate, or line of code every single day. By obsessing over subtraction rather than addition, he dramatically minimized hardware failure rates, boosted power efficiency, and revolutionized computing history.

In today's post, we examine the cognitive neuroscience behind Steve Wozniak's circuit minimalism habit and present a 3-step practical guide to streamlining complex systems and eliminating cognitive overload.

## 1. Breaking the Additive Bias: 'Subtractive Behavior' and Cognitive Load Theory

The human brain possesses an innate 'Additive Bias', defaulting to adding new elements when solving problems. However, accumulation overloads the <strong>Working Memory</strong> with <strong>Extraneous Cognitive Load</strong>. Practicing <strong>'Subtractive Behavior'</strong>—removing one component daily like Wozniak—reduces system complexity, suppresses error rates, and frees up resources in the <strong>Prefrontal Cortex</strong> to focus exclusively on core objectives.

## 3. Rule: Preserve Core Protocols While Pruning Non-Essential Layers

The primary error in minimalist refactoring is mindless deletion that compromises essential functionality. Like Wozniak, first gain a complete architectural grasp of the core protocol, then identify the most direct, elegant path to achieve identical or superior results.
