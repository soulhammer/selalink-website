"A novel is not written linearly from start to finish. Within the complete picture existing in my mind, I merely fill in the empty puzzle pieces one by one using cards." 🃏

Vladimir Nabokov (1899–1977), who showcased the pinnacle of 20th-century English literature with exquisite structures and elegant prose in masterpieces like 'Lolita' and 'Pale Fire'. Born into an aristocratic family in Imperial Russia and exiled to Europe and America following the Russian Revolution, he was a genius novelist who dominated both English and Russian literature, as well as a distinguished entomologist specializing in butterflies.

Astonishingly, Nabokov did not write his novels sequentially from the first page. Instead, he noted down fragments of dialogue, vivid descriptions, and plot ideas on 3x5-inch index cards as they came to mind. He spread these cards across his desk, freely shuffling and rearranging their sequence, and even carried cards with him in his car or while collecting butterflies to capture fleeting thoughts. From a brain science perspective, we investigate why this modular recording method is the ultimate strategy for complex projects and writing.

When tasked with writing a major report, proposal, or article, we typically face a blank screen and attempt to fill it linearly from sentence one to the end. However, this linear approach causes severe mental stress to the brain. Attempting to assemble logical structure, ensure contextual flow, and draft detailed prose simultaneously overloads the prefrontal cortex, leading to quick exhaustion and procrastination. Nabokov cleverly bypassed this limitation by physically fragmenting his thoughts.

In today's post, we examine how Vladimir Nabokov's index-card planning ritual liberates the brain's cognitive system and present a 3-step practical framework for modern professionals to successfully execute complex tasks and architecture designs.

## Neural Mechanisms of Blocking Cognitive Load and Combinatorial Creativity

When the brain perceives that it must produce an important and highly polished output, the amygdala triggers an unconscious alarm regarding potential failure, causing procrastination. By first engaging in low-stakes, trivial writing, feedback is sent to the brain that it is in an 'unevaluated, safe state,' calming amygdala hyperactivity. Simultaneously, Broca's area and the motor cortex are lightly activated, engaging the cognitive gears and drastically lowering the barrier of resistance when initiating the main task.

---

## Cautions and Tips

If you become overly absorbed in card writing and simply jot down ideas randomly without relevance, you will face a massive barrier of fragmentation when attempting to weave them together later. Therefore, each card must record only a single self-contained thought or episode, and you should periodically recall the overall architecture (e.g., full outline) to categorize cards into rough groupings.
