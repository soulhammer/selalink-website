"ACCURACY, ACCURACY, and again ACCURACY!" 📰

Joseph Pulitzer, the titan of journalism who established the philosophy and ethical foundation of modern media by founding the Pulitzer Prizes and endowing the Columbia University Graduate School of Journalism. Amidst the chaos of overflowing information and sensational rumors at the New York World newsroom desk, how did he maintain supreme journalistic credibility and public trust throughout his life? 🔍

Pulitzer kept the word "ACCURACY" posted directly on his desk and strictly adhered to a 3-step review routine every morning before approving stories for print. Reading through manuscripts at his desk, he asked three core questions: "1. Does this story have reliable evidence and verifiable data?", "2. Are subjective speculation and sensational bias completely excluded?", "3. Has direct cross-verification with involved parties and opposing views taken place?". This meticulous morning review routine served as an impenetrable firewall guarding journalistic integrity against misinformation.

In today's post, we explore the cognitive psychological principles of cognitive bias inhibition and System 2 logical processing embedded in Joseph Pulitzer's morning 3-step fact-checking routine.

## 1. Psychology of System 2 Questioning and Prefrontal Error Verification

The human brain relies heavily on intuitive, biased System 1 thinking to save energy. Uncritically consuming information easily leads to confirmation bias and cognitive distortion.

Systematically asking three structured verification questions—as Pulitzer did—forces the brain's System 2 logical network into gear. This activates the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) and prefrontal error-monitoring circuits, filtering out subjective assumptions and sharpening executive control to isolate objective facts.

## 3. Guide to Fact-Checking in Modern Information Processing and Decision-Making

Avoid passively consuming the flood of news or opinions. The key is setting up an intentional cognitive filtering system before submitting critical reports or making decisions by running them through three verification questions.
