"Let go of subjective expectations and allow the phenomena to speak for themselves." 🔬

In 1895, Wilhelm Röntgen opened a new chapter in medicine and physics by discovering X-rays—invisible high-energy electromagnetic waves—and was awarded the first Nobel Prize in Physics. How did he arrive at precise scientific truth without falling prey to subjective bias or sensory illusions? 💡

Röntgen was acutely cautious about subtle sensory noise or expectation bias contaminating data interpretation. While experimenting inside his darkroom, he recorded no subjective impressions or speculation, observing visible phenomena in absolute stillness. Only after exiting the darkroom into his well-lit study did he calmly reconstruct the experiment in his log using purely measured figures and objective data. Physically separating the moment of observation from the moment of interpretation was his secret to blocking confirmation bias.

In today's post, we explore the cognitive psychological principles of confirmation bias prevention and prefrontal objective data processing embedded in Nobel Laureate Wilhelm Röntgen's blind review routine.

## 1. Science of Blocking Confirmation Bias and Objective Prefrontal Processing

The human brain is highly vulnerable to confirmation bias, selectively gathering information that aligns with pre-existing beliefs. Attempting to interpret observations in real time easily distorts reality through subjective assumptions.

Separating the phase of sensory observation from data interpretation—as Röntgen did—inhibits real-time emotional bias and activates logical reasoning networks. This serves as a cognitive buffer that prevents subjective errors and grounds decisions strictly in verified facts.

## 3. Guide to Objective Data Review in Modern Work and Study

Avoid attaching emotional interpretations or assumptions to events as they unfold. The key is separating the time of observation from review, evaluating situations using measurable metrics and verified facts.
