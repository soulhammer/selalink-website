Joan Didion, the legendary essayist and novelist who captured America's inner loss and changing eras with sharp insight and stylish prose. Did you know that her elegant sentences and complex plot structures were forged in the subconscious mind of the night? 🛏️

Whenever Didion ran into a knot in her writing or couldn't find a breakthrough, she performed a curious sleep ritual. She took the printed draft she was writing into her bedroom and placed it on a nightstand right next to her bed. She explained, "I must sleep in the same room with the manuscript to breathe the same air as the book. That way, the thread of the story flowing in my mind doesn't break, allowing me to wake up in the morning with new connections." Indeed, upon waking, she frequently found solutions to tangled contexts as if by magic.

Today, BuildSelf explores the neural science of Didion's sleep routine that reorganizes cognitive structures overnight and suggests how modern people can solve unsolved dilemmas or plans using their sleep time.

## 1. REM Sleep Information Integration and Cognitive Priming

While awake, the brain employs the prefrontal cortex's logical and analytical control system to think along established schemas. When this conscious control is too strong, novel and creative associations are unlikely to occur.

Focusing on a problem or project right before falling asleep acts as <strong>'cognitive priming.'</strong> During REM sleep, the brain connects the amygdala and hippocampus, which govern emotional memory, and randomly cross-links knowledge fragments gathered during the day. Keeping the manuscript in the bedroom acts as a prompt for the sleeping brain to prioritize solving that specific problem. The subconscious processing running overnight hands us brilliant solutions upon waking in the morning.

## 3. Reading on tablet or smartphone screens destroys sleep quality
<div class="my-6 p-5 rounded-2xl border-l-4 border-indigo-500 bg-indigo-500/5 dark:bg-indigo-500/10 flex items-start gap-4">
  <span class="text-xl">💡</span>
  <div>
    <p class="text-slate-700 dark:text-slate-300 text-sm m-0 leading-relaxed">
      Reviewing documents on smartphone screens while lying in bed fools the brain into thinking it is daytime, suppressing melatonin and disrupting sleep stages. When doing priming training, always use paper prints or a handwritten notebook, or close your eyes and visualize the problem, to ensure you enter deep sleep and harvest the REM benefits.
