Milton Friedman (1912–2006) was the titan of 20th-century free-market economics and recipient of the 1976 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences. Leading the Chicago School of Economics, he established monetary theory and authored seminal works such as *A Monetary History of the United States, 1867–1960* and *Capitalism and Freedom*, warning against government overreach and erecting the framework for modern market economics. 📊✒️

Behind his legendary debater's precision and logical dominance lay his daily morning 'Counter-Argument Note' routine. Before finalizing his own positions, Friedman wrote down the key arguments of opposing Keynesian and state-interventionist scholars in his notebook. Instead of committing the 'Strawman' fallacy of diluting or mocking his opponents' views, he practiced strict 'Steelmanning'—reconstructing their positions into their most powerful, persuasive forms. Only after building the strongest possible counter-case did he systematically dissect and refute it line by line to fortify his own theories.

In today's post, we dissect the cognitive neuroscience behind Friedman's Steelmanning habit, exploring how active counter-argumentation inhibits confirmation bias and expands metacognitive flexibility.

## 1. Suppressing Confirmation Bias and Maximizing Dorsolateral Prefrontal (dlPFC) Cognitive Flexibility

The human brain is naturally susceptible to <strong>Confirmation Bias</strong> and <strong>MySide Bias</strong>, instinctively seeking information that reinforces pre-existing beliefs. Friedman's act of <strong>'Steelmanning'</strong>—actively constructing the strongest opposing arguments—strongly stimulates the brain's <strong>Dorsolateral Prefrontal Cortex (dlPFC)</strong> and <strong>Anterior Cingulate Cortex (ACC)</strong>. This neural engagement suppresses default heuristic bias and enhances <strong>Metacognition</strong>, allowing one to objectively audit logical flaws and build profound cognitive flexibility.

## 3. Caution: Avoiding 'Strawmanning' Fallacies and Emotional Reactivity

The most common trap when drafting counter-arguments is 'strawmanning'—deliberately weakening the opposing argument to make it easy to defeat. Unless you genuinely reconstruct your opponent's case in its most formidable form, you lose the neurological benefits of cognitive flexibility training. Approach counter-arguments with emotional detachment and strict objectivity.
