"To raise the quality of design, one needs quiet time to simplify and observe objects." 🏊‍♂️

"Less, but better." As chief designer at Braun, Dieter Rams revolutionized 20th-century industrial design and inspired legends like Apple's Jony Ive. Where did his genius ideas—visualizing a complex world into simple, functional products—come from?

Dieter Rams started every day with a morning swim to awaken his brain physically and relax his body. After swimming, he returned to his tranquil garden to prune bonsai trees and tend to his plants. Rather than trapping himself in complex planning or artificial shapes, he quietly observed the organic, essential structures of growing garden plants, formulating his core minimalist philosophy of eliminating unnecessary elements.

Today's post examines the cognitive science behind Dieter Rams' morning swim and observation routine and how modern people can apply it to maintain all-day focus.

## The 'Attention Restoration Theory' That Cleanses the Visual Cortex

Right upon waking, modern people are exposed to massive artificial visual stimuli like smartphones, notifications, and street signs. This rapidly drains the prefrontal cortex's directed attention capacity, causing cognitive fatigue before noon.

Dieter Rams' morning routine combined aerobic exercise with Attention Restoration Theory (ART) through nature observation. Observing subtle natural patterns like leaf veins and branch splits engages 'soft fascination'—a state requiring no mental strain. This allows directed attention networks to rest, dramatically restoring cognitive stamina for demanding design work.

## Artificial Visual Emptying Fills Your Mind with Ideas

Good design means an essential state with all unnecessary elements removed. Dieter Rams tended bonsai and observed nature every morning to empty artificial stimuli from his brain and create space for essential thoughts. Cleanse your attention filter with brief morning nature observation.
