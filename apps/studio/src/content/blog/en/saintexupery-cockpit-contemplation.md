"Perfection is achieved, not when there is nothing more to add, but when there is nothing left to take away." ✈️

Antoine de Saint-Exupéry (1900–1944) was a master of 20th-century French literature and a legendary pioneer of airmail aviation. Amidst the turmoil of World Wars I and II, he piloted mail planes across the Sahara Desert and the Andes Mountains, writing timeless classics such as 'The Little Prince', 'Night Flight', and 'Wind, Sand and Stars'.

In the 1930s, an airplane cockpit was a space of extreme solitude, filled with noise, vibration, and freezing cold. Yet for Saint-Exupéry, high above the earth, the cockpit served as a noble creative sanctuary where he gazed at the vast night sky, stars, and clouds to purify his soul. During flights, he densely recorded surging thoughts and sentences in a small leather notebook.

Upon landing, as he transferred his notes into manuscripts, he became captivated by the habit of mercilessly paring away unnecessary embellishments and emotional adjectives. His legendary maxim—'Perfection is achieved, not when there is nothing more to add, but when there is nothing left to take away'—was the fruit of brain metacognition born from high-altitude solitude and ground-level subtraction.

## 1. Why Solitude and the Subtraction Habit Maximize Prefrontal Metacognition

Neuroscience research shows that a state of solitude isolated from external noise activates the brain's Default Mode Network (DMN), facilitating creative perception. Subsequently, the act of paring away unnecessary elements or rhetoric stimulates prefrontal metacognition, elevating thought precision.

## 3. Saint-Exupéry Habit Cautions and Refining Tips

Do not censor or edit during the idea generation phase. Allow ideas to flow freely in solitude first, then follow a two-step process in the review phase by boldly trimming over 30% of unnecessary modifiers.
