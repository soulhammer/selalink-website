"When I lost my sight, I only then began to explore the inner world and the true labyrinths." 📚

Jorge Luis Borges (1899–1986) was an Argentine literary giant who possessed one of the most original minds in 20th-century literature. Having pioneered postmodernism and speculative fiction through masterpieces such as 'Ficciones' and 'The Aleph', tragedy struck him early in life. Due to hereditary glaucoma, his vision progressively deteriorated from his thirties, leaving him completely blind by 1955—the very year he was appointed Director of the National Library of Argentina. Surrounded by hundreds of thousands of books he could no longer read, he faced an agonizing irony.

Yet Borges refused to succumb to despair or abandon creation. Instead of writing on paper, he honed a 'Mental Dictation' routine, transforming his inner mind into a massive 3D architecture. He systematically arranged sentences, logical flows, and literary structures onto an imaginary mental chalkboard and spatial labyrinth. After completing all edits and structural adjustments purely in his head, he dictated the polished prose aloud to his mother, Leonor Acevedo, or to assistants. This extraordinary practice stretched his visuospatial working memory to its absolute limits, becoming a celebrated example of neuroplasticity in cognitive neuroscience.

In today's post, we analyze the brain science underlying Jorge Luis Borges's 'mental dictation and 3D spatial visualization' routine and present a 3-step practical guide for modern knowledge workers to boost working memory capacity and mental focus.

## 1. Cross-Modal Plasticity and Visuospatial Working Memory Expansion Principles

When external visual input is restricted, the brain recruits the visual cortex to process auditory, tactile, and spatial information—a phenomenon known as 'Cross-Modal Plasticity'. When the blind Borges mentally assembled intricate labyrinths and text structures, his visual cortex operated as an association area dedicated to high-level logical design and spatial layout. Mentally visualizing 3D abstract concept maps with closed eyes dramatically expands the visuospatial working memory capacity between the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex, unlocking advanced intuition to assemble complex, fragmented data into coherent frameworks.

## 3. Caution & Tips: Focusing on Organic Interstructures Rather Than Text Symbols

The core of the Borgesian visualization routine is not reproducing text fonts or letter spellings behind your eyelids. It is drawing a '3D structural framework' (mind maps, spatial organograms, or 3D labyrinth models) showing how key concepts connect and interact. Focus on abstract causal relationships and organic structures rather than literal letter shapes to maximize neuroplastic benefits for visuospatial working memory.
