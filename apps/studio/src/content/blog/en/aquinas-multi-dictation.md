"Faith without reason is blind, and reason without faith is powerless." 📜

Thomas Aquinas, the master of medieval Scholastic philosophy known as the 'Doctor Angelicus', who left behind immortal masterpieces in intellectual history including the 'Summa Theologiae'. Amid monastic solitude and immense academic pressure, what was his secret to completing an enormous intellectual system?

Aquinas's speed of thought far outpaced the speed of handwriting. To fully record the ideas surging from deep within his mind, he kept 3 to 4 scribes in his room, dictating manuscripts on entirely different subjects simultaneously. Pacing the room, he would utter a metaphysical hypothesis to the first scribe, then turn seamlessly to the second scribe to construct an ethical argument, and proceed to explain biblical commentary to the third scribe with flawless logical coherence. This astonishing focus—switching from one topic to another without ever losing previous context—was a historical demonstration of working memory pushed to its extreme.

In today's post, we analyze the neuroscientific principles of working memory partitioning and sequential high-executive control embedded in Thomas Aquinas's multi-dictation method, and present a 3-step practical guide for modern individuals to master complex multi-projects without confusion.

## 1. Brain Cognitive Principles of Working Memory Partitioning

Coordinating multiple abstract tasks simultaneously usually generates severe information interference and cognitive task-switching costs in the cerebrum. However, Thomas Aquinas overcame this through 'Working Memory Partitioning'. By highly training the Central Executive System of the prefrontal cortex, he maintained the knowledge nodes of each project active in separate compartments. This allowed him to rapidly switch context while preventing cognitive depletion and information overlap.

## 2. 3-Step Practical Routine for Modern Professionals

    Select 2 to 3 different projects that currently require deep thinking. Choosing 4 or more topics exceeds the information capacity of the prefrontal cortex and causes cognitive interference, so limiting the number of targets is recommended.

## 3. Beware of Unproductive Multitasking

Do not confuse this habit with modern, distractible multitasking (e.g., coding while reading emails). Simple distraction severely drops work quality and speed. Aquinas's method is Sequential Execution of single, highly focused tasks divided into partitions. In other words, when processing one object, you must pour 100% of your brain resources into it.
