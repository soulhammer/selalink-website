"Do one thing every day that scares you." 🚶‍♂️

Eleanor Roosevelt (1884–1962) was a monumental American diplomat, activist, and First Lady who comforted Americans through the Great Depression and World War II and chaired the drafting of the UN Universal Declaration of Human Rights. Amidst intense public scrutiny and political turmoil, her secret to maintaining composed inner peace lay in her nightly writing routine. ✍️

From 1935 until her passing in 1962—spanning 27 years—she wrote a daily newspaper column titled 'My Day' six days a week without fail. This was not a grandiose political manifesto; rather, it was an intimate journal sharing encounters with marginalized citizens, visits to public works sites, and quiet personal reflections. By sitting at her desk with a pen each evening, she stepped back from the storm of daily events to observe and objectify her mind.

In today's post, we share the neuroscience benefits of Eleanor Roosevelt's lifelong daily journaling routine and present a 3-step reflective writing method to clarify values and manage mental resilience.

## 1. Consolidation of Memory and Promotion of Cognitive Metacognition

When experiences and emotions during the day are left unorganized, they overstimulate the amygdala, turning into stress memories or consuming working memory capacity. Structuring the day's flow through writing activates the <strong>Hippocampus</strong>, transferring episodic memories into stable long-term memory. Furthermore, translating events and emotions into objective words stimulates the frontal cortex, dramatically boosting <strong>Metacognition</strong>—the ability to view oneself from a 3rd-person perspective, tempering emotional impulsivity.

## 3. Maintain Contemplative Calmness Over Idealized Goals

Forcing toxic positivity or recording heavy, unachievable plans creates cognitive stress and sabotages journaling. As Eleanor did, calmly review physical facts and encounters, recording your internal state naturally with comfortable ease.
