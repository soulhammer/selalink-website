"Even time spent on horseback during marches is a high-level cognitive space to train thought and design the future." 🐎

Simón Bolívar, known as 'El Libertador' for liberating six South American nations (Colombia, Venezuela, Ecuador, Peru, Bolivia, Panama) from Spanish rule, shaped world history. Amid demanding military marches across the Andes and thousands of kilometers on horseback, the secret behind his eloquent prose and strategic governance lay in a routine that converted travel time into intellectual capital.

Bolívar never wasted time in the saddle during long-distance marches. Holding history or philosophy books (such as Montesquieu and Rousseau) in one hand, or dictating diplomatic correspondence and epistles to secretaries riding alongside him, he crafted numerous policy letters and philosophical essays while riding on horseback.

This routine was a powerful time-utilization strategy that transformed physical travel into a locus of high-level cognitive input and output. In today's post, we introduce the cognitive neuroscience principles of Dead-Time Cognitive Restructuring and Verbal Externalization embedded in Bolívar's horseback reading and dictation routine.

## 1. Strengthening Working Memory Through Dead-Time Cognitive Restructuring and Verbal Externalization

Applying continuous intellectual stimulation during automated physical activities like riding or marching induces <strong>'Dead-Time Cognitive Restructuring'</strong> in the brain. This transforms otherwise wasted time into high-level cognitive input, maximizing neural availability.

Furthermore, dictating thoughts aloud acts as <strong>'Verbal Externalization'</strong>, simultaneously stimulating the brain's language cortex and prefrontal executive circuits. Structuring thoughts into spoken language for immediate output expands Working Memory and refines logical precision.
