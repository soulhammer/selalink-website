In 1927, Charles Lindbergh flew a single-seat propeller plane, 'Spirit of St. Louis,' from New York across the Atlantic to Paris. In an era without radar or smart navigation, how did he survive the extreme stress of solo flight and sleep deprivation for 33.5 hours without a single mechanical or instrument error? The strength came from a single sheet of paper. ✈️

Lindbergh believed that relying solely on his memory to check fuel valves, barometric readings, and engine oil flow was cognitive suicide. He designed written checklists for takeoff, flight segments, and landing. Attaching the checklist to the cockpit, he kept a strict routine of tracing each item with his finger and checking them off while reading aloud. This structural check saved his life and revolutionized safety systems in aviation and medicine.

Today, BuildSelf analyzes how Lindbergh's checklist routine relieves the brain's working memory overload and prevents mistakes, and proposes checklist methods for modern business projects or public presentations.

## 1. Cognitive Fatigue Prevention and Omission Error Prevention Mechanism

When we process sequential tasks under stress, the prefrontal cortex consumes massive energy managing working memory. To conserve energy, the brain automatically skips or rationalizes certain steps, which is the source of fatal 'omission errors.'

Tracing a written checklist with your eyes and fingers like Lindbergh preserves the energy the brain would spend trying to remember details. Instead of retrieving memory, the brain focuses its energy entirely on <strong>'executive control,'</strong> allowing it to prevent mistakes under severe stress and maintain a solid quality of work.

## 3. Scanning loosely with your eyes cannot help the brain
