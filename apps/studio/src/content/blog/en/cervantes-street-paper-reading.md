"I am so fond of reading that I will read even the torn papers in the streets." 📜

Miguel de Cervantes (1547–1616), the Spanish literary giant who authored 'Don Quixote', created the foundational masterpiece of the modern novel. Despite enduring tragic misfortunes—a war injury that crippled his left hand, five years of slavery in Algiers, and multiple unjust imprisonments—how did he build such vast, multifaceted humanistic brilliance without a university degree?

Cervantes was an obsessive, omnivorous reader who picked up and read any scrap of paper with text lying in the streets—torn receipts, discarded letters, or flyers. In fact, within 'Don Quixote', he famously incorporated the meta-fictional premise that he found the original manuscript of Don Quixote's story on torn Arabic papers bought from a boy in a Toledo market. This relentless habit of absorbing random, unpredictable texts liberated his brain from mental ruts, giving birth to a revolutionary novel that seamlessly blended classic tragedy, slapstick comedy, and popular culture.

In today's post, we examine the cognitive mechanisms behind Cervantes' street paper reading routine and explore a practical guide for modern individuals to break free from filter bubbles and trigger creative serendipity.

## 1. Random Stimuli to Break Mental Ruts and Fuel 'Divergent Thinking'

When you consume only tailored information recommended by algorithms, your brain enters <strong>'Mental Ruts'</strong>—processing biased, predictable data. Encountering random, unexpected texts—like paper scraps on the street—forces the brain to initiate <strong>'Divergent Thinking'</strong>, connecting previously unrelated fragments. This provides fresh cognitive stimulation to the <strong>Prefrontal Cortex</strong>, maximizing <strong>Serendipity</strong>—the ability to make valuable, unexpected discoveries.

## 3. Rule: Active Interconnection Over Passive Scrolling

The crucial rule when adopting a random reading routine is avoiding passive doomscrolling or mindless data consumption. Cervantes actively connected random fragments to his ongoing literary ideas. When encountering unfamiliar random text, ask yourself: 'How can this unexpected piece of information connect to the problem or project I am currently working on?' Forced connection is the key to creative breakthroughs.
