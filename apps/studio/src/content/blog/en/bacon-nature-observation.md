"Nature, to be commanded, must be obeyed." 🌿

Francis Bacon (1561–1626), Lord Chancellor and politician of England, and the philosopher who challenged medieval Scholastic authority to lay the foundations of empiricism and the modern scientific method (induction). Having redefined the structure of human intellect through works like 'The Advancement of Learning' and 'Novum Organum', how did he maintain refreshing mental clarity amid intense political intrigue and heavy state responsibilities?

Bacon was a passionate garden enthusiast and a meticulous observer of nature. Early every morning, he walked into his garden to closely observe seasonal shifts, plant growth processes, weather, and soil conditions, dictating his emerging thoughts and observations to a secretary by his side. For him, this morning observation walk in nature was far more than an academic pursuit; it was a 'brain detox' ritual that completely reset his cerebrum, exhausted by political pressure and complex cognitive load. The crisp morning air, the visual sense of liberation offered by vibrant natural surroundings, and relaxed observation awakened his brain, sharpening the keen insights of his inductive philosophy.

In today's post, we analyze the cognitive science principles of Attention Restoration Theory and brain fatigue resetting embedded in Francis Bacon's 'morning garden observation walk' routine, presenting a 3-step practical action plan for modern individuals to restore attention and refresh their brains in daily life.

## 1. Why Nature Observation Walks Restore Attentional Focus

Modern tasks require artificial 'Directed Attention', easily fatiguing the prefrontal cortex. According to Attention Restoration Theory in environmental psychology, natural stimuli like rustling leaves or sunlight filtering through trees induce 'Soft Fascination'. This shifts attention naturally without forcing brain power, allowing the exhausted prefrontal cognitive control network to rest and recharge, thereby maximizing inductive thinking and creative problem-solving.

## 3. Complete Offlining of Digital Stimuli

Taking photos with a smartphone, replying to text messages, or listening to informative podcasts while walking continues to consume the brain's directed attention. To gain the attention restoration effect, set your smartphone to silent and isolate it completely. Immerse yourself solely in offline sensory stimuli.
