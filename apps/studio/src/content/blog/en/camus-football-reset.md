Albert Camus, the novelist who won the Nobel Prize for 'The Stranger' and 'The Plague.' Where did he find the energy to escape mental suffering and continue writing while battling chronic tuberculosis and writer's block? It came from the muddy playing fields. ⚽

Camus played soccer in the poor alleys of Algiers in his youth, later starring as the main goalkeeper for his university team. Even after achieving fame as a writer, he famously declared, "All that I know most surely about morality and obligations, I owe to football." Whenever writer's block or existential dread hit, he dropped his pen and stood in front of the goalposts. Throwing his body to block fast-flying balls and getting covered in dust forced his overloaded frontal lobe to quiet down, recharging his life energy.

Today, BuildSelf explores the neurological benefits of Camus' soccer reset habit and introduces a dynamic body-focus guide for modern people to immediately escape burnout.

## 1. Forced Frontal Lobe Offline and Cerebellum Cognitive Switching

Complex intellectual tasks like writing, planning, or accounting severely wear down the prefrontal cortex. When prefrontal energy is depleted, we suffer from indecision, anxiety, and chronic fatigue (burnout).

Albert Camus' goalkeeper action of blocking fast-flying balls puts the prefrontal reflection circuitry completely offline. This is because it instantly switches the brain's active regions to the <strong>'cerebellum'</strong> and motor cortex, which manage reflexes and body coordination. This rapid cognitive switching grants physiological rest to the exhausted frontal lobe, dropping cortisol levels and restoring mental energy so you can find fresh inspiration when returning to the desk.
