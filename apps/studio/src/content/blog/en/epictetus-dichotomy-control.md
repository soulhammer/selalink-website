"Freedom is the only worthy goal in life. It is won by disregarding things that lie beyond our control." 📜

Epictetus (c. 50–135 AD) was born into bitter slavery and left permanently lame from his master's abuse. Yet, through unyielding rationality, he achieved complete mental freedom, establishing a renowned Stoic academy in Nicopolis after being exiled from Rome. His teachings deeply inspired Emperor Marcus Aurelius's *Meditations*.

Though Epictetus wrote nothing himself, his student Arrian transcribed his lectures into the *Discourses* and compiled the *Enchiridion*. At the core of Epictetus's philosophy was a nightly reflective routine built on the 'Dichotomy of Control'. Every evening, he evaluated his daily experiences by dividing them into two strict categories: things 'up to us' (our thoughts, desires, actions, and judgments) and things 'not up to us' (other people's opinions, external outcomes, health, and past events). He recognized that suffering begins the exact moment we attach our happiness to things outside our authority.

In today's post, we unpack the cognitive neuroscience behind Epictetus's 'dichotomy of control' evening reflection and present a practical 3-step action plan to eliminate helplessness and anxiety.

## Internalizing the Locus of Control and Downregulating Amygdala Cortisol Responses

Chronic stress and anxiety stem primarily from perceived helplessness when the brain hyper-focuses on external variables it cannot control (such as other people's reactions, market conditions, or past events). Epictetus's Dichotomy of Control shifts your 'Locus of Control' from external outcomes to internal agency. By formally acknowledging and releasing uncontrollable variables, the prefrontal cortex (PFC) exerts top-down executive regulation over the limbic system. This calms hyperactivation in the amygdala—the brain's threat center—dramatically reducing cortisol levels and restoring emotional equilibrium.

---

## Caution: Your Jurisdiction Ends at Your Own Actions

Blaming yourself when external responses or market outcomes turn unfavorable—despite giving your best effort—pushes the brain into severe ego depletion and depressive cycles. Understand that your sole duty ends at your intentional effort; the ultimate outcome belongs to the universe.
