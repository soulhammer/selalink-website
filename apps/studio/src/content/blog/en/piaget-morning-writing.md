"Intelligence is not what we know, but what we do when we don't know." 🚶‍♂️

Jean Piaget (1896–1980), the titan of developmental psychology who redefined how humanity understands cognitive growth in children. Behind his colossal academic achievements lay a unique dual-rhythm habit that structured his thinking. 📝

Every morning, Piaget sat at his desk early to write at least four pages using only pen and paper. This morning writing was not aimed at producing polished prose for publication, but at expressing fragmented thoughts physically to clear working memory. Once his cognitive resources were unloaded, he spent hours every afternoon walking along peaceful Swiss lakes and mountain paths. Remarkably, with each rhythmic step, the complex concepts written in the morning organically harmonized and crystallized within his mind.

In today's post, we analyze the neuroscientific mechanics behind Piaget's morning writing and afternoon contemplative walking, offering a practical framework to elevate productivity and creative insight.

## 1. Working Memory Externalization and Default Mode Network Synchronization

Relying purely on mental effort to solve complex problems overloads limited working memory capacity. Piaget's morning writing served as <strong>'Working Memory Externalization'</strong>, offloading mental burden onto paper.

The subsequent afternoon walk stimulates Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor (BDNF) through physical movement while engaging the Default Mode Network (DMN). During this incubation phase, knowledge fragments recorded in the morning spontaneously recombine in the subconscious into rigorous logical structures.

## 3. Balance Information Input with Dedicated Output and Mental Ventilation

Modern life floods the brain with constant smartphone input, while depriving it of expressive output and contemplative rest. Balance your daily rhythm like Piaget—combining morning expressive writing (output) with afternoon peaceful walking (ventilation) to grant your brain space to process.
