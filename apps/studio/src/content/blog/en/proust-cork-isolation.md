Marcel Proust, the French novelist who revolutionized 20th-century fiction and demonstrated the peak of sensory description. He suffered from chronic asthma and severe neurasthenia throughout his life, particularly sensory hypersensitivity that reacted neurotically to minute light and sound. Consequently, he remodeled his bedroom into a complete soundproof fortress to escape Paris's noisy environment.

Proust lined all the walls of his bedroom with thick cork panels to completely block sound. He hung layered heavy curtains to prevent even a single beam of light from entering, and wrote lying in bed under a dim candle. In this space that obsessively cut off all external artificial sensations, Proust was able to perfectly recall memories from decades ago that slept in his subconscious, triggered by a tiny sensory cue like the smell of a madeleine cake dipped in tea.

Today, BuildSelf explores the neuroscientific mechanisms behind Marcel Proust's 'cork sensory isolation' habit and how modern people can adapt it to shut off external distractions.

## 1. The Effect of 'Thalamus Gating' Preventing Random Information Overload

The <strong>thalamus</strong>, the gateway in our brain that transfers sensory information to consciousness, consumes substantial energy classifying information every time surrounding noise or smartphone light enters. This depletes the frontal lobe's cognitive executive resources, causing distractions.

When you block artificial stimuli at the source by creating a physical cork-soundproofed and dark environment like Proust, the brain saves all attention-focusing energy used for information filtering and channels it into internal reflection and cognitive processing. In this state, the hippocampus, which retrieves past memories, and the association cortex, which assists in memory integration, are activated to their maximum, forming a brain highway that vividly pulls long-term memories from the subconscious without forced effort.
