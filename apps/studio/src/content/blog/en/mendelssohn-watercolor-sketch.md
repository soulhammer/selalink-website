"The thoughts which are expressed to me by music are not too indefinite to be put into words, but on the contrary, too definite; and the colors at the tip of the brush provide a comfortable haven for melodies." 🎨

Felix Mendelssohn (1809–1847) was the German composer, pianist, and conductor who led the peak of 19th-century Romantic music with lyrical masterworks such as 'A Midsummer Night's Dream', 'Violin Concerto in E minor', and the 'Italian Symphony'. While constantly exhausted by demanding orchestration and rehearsals, he possessed another astonishing artistic talent.

Between intense music sessions, Mendelssohn regularly walked outdoors carrying a portable sketchbook, a box of watercolor paints, and brushes. He meticulously captured windswept hills, quaint Swiss villages, and sunlit Italian landscapes on paper. Leaving behind over 300 masterly watercolors during his lifetime, painting was not a mere pastime for him, but a sophisticated cognitive reset routine—shifting sensory channels from auditory processing to visual expression. This watercolor routine prevented mental fatigue and provided a fresh oasis of inspiration for his brain.

In this post, we explore the cognitive science of cross-sensory shifting and right-brain creativity activation embedded in Mendelssohn's watercolor routine.

## 1. The 'Cross-Modal Association' Effect That Spans Across the Senses

When we paint, the brain closely engages the visual cortex and spatial cognition regions to perceive shapes accurately. According to cognitive neuroscience research, the phenomenon where activation in one sensory domain enhances creativity in another is called <strong>'Cross-modal Association'</strong>.

As Felix Mendelssohn observed landscapes every morning and captured color and form in watercolors, his brain's visuospatial processing circuits were maximized. This unique sensory activation sequentially synchronized associative circuits dealing with auditory processing and temporal arts, helping enrich harmonic structure and rhythmic organization in his music. Using an alternative sense for creative expression prevents fatigue in the primary neural circuits and purifies creative inspiration.

## 3. Skill Improvement Is Not the Goal; Simple Relaxation and Sensory Refreshment Matter Most

The reason we sketch is not to become professional artists. It is to grant rest to the frontal lobe weary from text, numbers, and logic, and to flexibly restore neural connectivity between sensory networks through non-verbal, visuo-tactile artistic movement. Do not worry about crooked lines; simply immerse yourself in the sensory experience.
