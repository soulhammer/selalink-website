"I keep track of the daily output on a large chart on the wall... so as not to fool myself." 📝

Ernest Hemingway (1899–1961) was the titan of American literature awarded both the Pulitzer Prize (1953) and the Nobel Prize in Literature (1954) for masterpieces such as *The Old Man and the Sea*, *A Farewell to Arms*, and *The Sun Also Rises*. Behind his famous, iceberg-style prose lay an uncompromising, quantitative tracking habit.

At his home, Finca Vigía in Cuba, Hemingway wrote standing up at a desk. At the end of every morning writing session, he meticulously counted the exact number of new words he had produced that day and recorded them on a large cardboard progress chart taped to the wall. As he famously revealed in his 1958 *Paris Review* interview with George Plimpton, recording daily numbers—whether 450, 575, 1,250, or merely 150 words—was essential to maintain absolute honesty with himself. Seeing visual, empirical proof of his labor provided the psychological fuel needed to resume writing the following morning.

In today's post, we unpack the cognitive neuroscience behind Ernest Hemingway's 'daily word count tracking' routine and present a practical guide to maximize personal performance.

## Self-Monitoring and the Neuroscience of the Dopamine Reward Circuit

Quantitatively recording and monitoring target behaviors is recognized in behavioral psychology as <strong>Self-Monitoring</strong>. When behavioral progress is visually quantified, the brain receives immediate empirical feedback, firing the <strong>Dopamine Reward Circuit</strong> across the striatum and nucleus accumbens. Honest daily output tracking converts abstract effort into visible achievement, signaling the <strong>Prefrontal Cortex</strong> to generate strong intrinsic motivation for subsequent execution. Hemingway intuitively leveraged this dopamine feedback loop to sustain unshakeable daily writing momentum.

---

## Caution: Prioritize the Act of Daily Honest Logging Over the Output Volume

Vague effort leads directly to vague results. The prefrontal cortex engages self-control only when performance is observed through exact numerical figures. Regardless of whether daily numbers are high or low, focus on leaving an honest mark on your progress chart every single day to maintain cognitive momentum.
