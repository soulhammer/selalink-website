"The fragments of life collected on the open road hold a deeper truth of the soul than sentences spun in a quiet study." 🌿

Walt Whitman, the father of modern American poetry and a great 19th-century pioneer of Anglo-American literature, shattered the constraints of traditional meter with his monumental masterpiece *Leaves of Grass*. Opening the horizon of Free Verse, he elevated democratic passion and the everyday lives of ordinary people into brilliant poetic language. His fluid poetic cadence and boundless breath served as the decisive beacon that emancipated American literature from European shadows.

The secret behind his ability to endlessly draw forth vibrant poetic inspiration and boundless vitality lay in his 'outdoor pocket scrap sketching' routine. Every day, Whitman walked the bustling streets of New York and tranquil country trails. Carrying scraps of paper cut to size and a pencil in his pocket, whenever a passerby's expression, a bus driver's fragment of dialogue, or the rustle of wind through leaves touched his heart, he sketched it down on the spot.

He never over-relied on memory, capturing raw sensory stimuli in their original form on paper scraps. In the evening, sitting in his study, he spread these memory fragments across his desk, painstakingly weaving them into grand narrative poetic lines. In today's post, we introduce the cognitive science principles of Embodied Cognition and the Incubation Effect embedded in Walt Whitman's walking note routine.

## 1. Mechanisms of Embodied Cognition and the Incubation Effect Transcending Creativity

Combining walking movement with the collection of outdoor sensory stimuli activates the brain's Embodied Cognition circuits, maximizing divergent thinking. As systemic blood flow increases and the cerebellum and motor cortex activate during a walk, cognitive rigidity in the prefrontal cortex relaxes, allowing sensitive capture of rich visual and auditory stimuli.

Furthermore, reviewing and integrating fragments collected on pocket scraps during the evening triggers the brain's Incubation Effect. Sensory stimuli matured in the subconscious and individual fragments captured throughout the day are reorganized into sophisticated, high-level poetic structures during evening refinement.

## 3. Avoid Over-Refining On-Site; Focus on Capturing Raw Sensory Prototypes

Whitman's walking notes were not meant for polishing perfect poetic lines on the spot, but for sketching raw sensory fragments. Trying to perfect phrasing or applying logical judgment during a walk disrupts observational flow and causes cognitive overload. The key is role division: rapidly record sensory raw material on-site, and reserve refinement and sentence completion for the study at night.
