Lee Jung-seob, the legendary master of modern Korean Western painting, suffered severe poverty and separation from his family during the Korean War. Lacking money for paint or canvas, he never stopped. Instead, he collected discarded cigarette packs to create drawings on the tin foil lining. 🐂

Scratching the aluminum surface with a stylus to engrave lines and wiping soot or paint into the grooves, he created art. Neuroscientifically, the high-tension tactile sensation of scratching metal surfaces blocks distractions and anchors focus in the present.

Today, BuildSelf uncovers the brain science principles of Lee Jung-seob's tin foil drawing routine, proposing a 3-step action plan for modern people to escape mental clutter and enter deep creative flow.

## 1. Tactile-Visual Associative Feedback and Attention Anchoring Through Tactile Arousal

In brain science, tactile stimulation activates primitive attention centers more strongly than visual or auditory cues. Scratching a stylus onto a foil surface triggers 'tactile arousal' via fingernail-tip receptors, waking the prefrontal lobe.

This multisensory loop—combining the sound of scraping metal, physical friction, and engraved visual depth—fully occupies working memory. It blocks the neural space where stress, depression, or anxiety is generated, leading to mindfulness and inner calm.
